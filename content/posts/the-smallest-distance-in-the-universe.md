*A journey from the Planck length to the question of what, or who, made it all*

It started with a question that sounded almost embarrassingly simple: what is the Planck length? The more questions followed, the more intrigued I became. How does it compare with an electron? Can scientists really study something that small? And do physicists ever ask questions as silly as mine? It turns out they do, every single day. Silly-sounding questions are often exactly where physics begins.

This text follows that trail, from the tiniest scale we can calculate to the biggest question anyone can ask. It also connects to the previous text, about superintelligence, because the last part imagines what such a machine might one day tell us.

**A note before we start.** I am a mechanical engineer, not a physicist. I am comfortable with some of the math, but a good part of what follows goes beyond what I fully understand. I follow my questions where they lead and check each claim against its sources. Where I understand something, I try to say it plainly. Where I am only passing on what scientists propose, or where I am guessing, I say that too.

## How small is small?

Start with what we know well. An atom is a small crowd: a dense nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by a cloud of far lighter electrons. Zoom in further and the protons and neutrons turn out to be built from even smaller particles called quarks. The electron is different. Physics treats it as a point particle, with no internal parts and possibly no size at all. Experiments say only that its radius cannot be larger than about 10⁻¹⁸ meters.

Now enters the Planck length: about 1.6 × 10⁻³⁵ meters. It is not measured with any ruler. It comes from combining three fundamental constants of nature: the speed of light, the Planck constant and the gravitational constant. It is roughly 10²⁰ times smaller than a proton. Even next to the electron's upper size limit, it is about 60 million billion times smaller, so you could line up some sixty quadrillion Planck lengths across the radius of a single electron. A queue that would make even the longest supermarket line look short.

Here is another way to feel it. If an electron were scaled up to the size of the Earth, the Planck length would still be only about as big as a single atom. Or take something you can hold in your hand: blow a single grain of sand, half a millimeter across, up until it is as wide as the entire observable universe, and on that scale the Planck length would be only about 30 micrometers, the width of a skin cell, barely visible to the naked eye.

| Object | Approximate size | Times larger than the Planck length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Planck length | 1.6 × 10⁻³⁵ m | 1 |
| Proton | 1.7 × 10⁻¹⁵ m | about 10²⁰ |
| Grain of sand | 5 × 10⁻⁴ m | about 3 × 10³¹ |
| Human being | 1.7 m | about 10³⁵ |
| Observable universe | 8.8 × 10²⁶ m | about 5 × 10⁶¹ |

The table hides a curious fact. We are closer in size to the entire observable universe than to the Planck length. From a human to the universe is a jump of about 10²⁶. From the Planck length up to a human is a jump of about 10³⁵. In scale terms, we are not in the middle. We are much nearer to the top, which is fascinating in itself.

## What happens to space and time down there?

Our everyday picture is simple: space is a smooth stage, and time is a steady clock. At the Planck scale, that picture is expected to fail. The uncertainty principle says that the smaller the region you examine, the wilder the energy fluctuations inside it. At the Planck scale they would be strong enough to distort the geometry of space and time itself. The physicist John Wheeler gave the result a memorable name: quantum foam, space-time as a boiling, bubbling surface instead of a smooth fabric. Picture a pot of water just before it boils: from a distance the surface looks calm, but up close it is churning, and no bubble lasts for long. At the Planck scale, space-time is expected to behave in a similar way. Some versions describe microscopic wormholes and tiny black holes flickering in and out of existence, and the ideas of "before and after" or "here and there" may stop meaning anything.

It is worth being clear that this is a theoretical picture. Nobody has observed quantum foam. It is what our best equations suggest when pushed beyond where they have been tested.

A related puzzle shows why the scale matters. Compress a particle down to the Planck length and, according to general relativity, it collapses into a black hole. At that size, two measures become equal: how blurry the particle is, because quantum rules stop us from pinning it to a single point, and how wide its black hole is, meaning the point of no return beyond which nothing can escape. This makes it roughly the smallest black hole that could exist, and it marks the exact crossroads where gravity and quantum mechanics collide. A fun detail: the mass at which this happens, the Planck mass, is about 22 millionths of a gram, around the weight of a speck of dust. That is absurdly heavy for a single particle.

## Do real scientists ask questions like this?

Yes. Entire fields of physics are devoted to this scale, even though nobody can look at it directly.

- **String theory** proposes that the basic ingredients of nature are not points but tiny vibrating loops of energy, strings, working at roughly the Planck scale.
- **Loop quantum gravity** suggests that space is woven out of tiny, discrete loops, so that there is a literal minimum "atom of space" that cannot be divided further.
- **Cosmology** studies relics of the early universe for hints of what happened when everything was compressed to extreme density.

Both string theory and loop quantum gravity are attempts to make gravity and quantum mechanics work together. Neither has been confirmed by experiment.

## How do you study something you can never see?

No microscope can look inside an electron, let alone reach the Planck length. Scientists use four routes instead.

- **Colliders.** At CERN, the Large Hadron Collider smashes protons together at 99.9999991 percent of the speed of light, and researchers study the debris. To resolve the Planck length directly, a collider would need to be roughly the size of the Milky Way. Since we cannot build that, colliders test the models that lead up to the boundary.
- **Telescopes.** The universe was once compressed to extreme density, so its oldest light carries a record of that time. The European Space Agency's Planck satellite mapped this light, the cosmic microwave background, between 2009 and 2013. Finding any trace of Planck-scale physics in it would be extraordinarily hard, and that search is highly speculative.
- **Gravitational waves.** The LIGO detectors (the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory, two giant instruments in the United States) use lasers over arms about four kilometers long to measure ripples in space-time from colliding black holes. They first detected such a signal in 2015, and the project won the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics. Each detection tells us a little more about how space-time behaves.
- **Mathematics.** When instruments run out of reach, theorists use equations as their microscope. Einstein's gravity works beautifully for stars, and quantum mechanics works beautifully for atoms, but when you try to combine them at the Planck scale, the math returns infinities, a sign that something is broken. Theorists spend careers trying to remove those infinities, and in doing so they predict subtle clues for experimenters to hunt.

How does anyone get access to such tools? No single scientist, university or even country owns them. CERN is funded by its member countries, and thousands of researchers share its equipment. To use a major space telescope, a scientist cannot simply pay a fee. They write a detailed proposal explaining what they want to look at and why it matters, and a panel of experts awards the observing time to the most promising ideas. Much of the data is later made public, which means a physicist sitting in a small room anywhere in the world can download observations from some of the most expensive instruments ever built. You have to write an essay to borrow the telescope, but once the data is out, anyone can use it.

## From the smallest question to the biggest one

A warning: from here on, I stop explaining and start wondering. What follows are ideas from scientists and philosophers that I find fascinating but cannot judge myself, so please read them as possibilities, not conclusions.

Here the trail turns. The same gap that hides the Planck scale, the missing bridge between gravity and quantum mechanics, also stands between us and the earliest instant of the universe. The Planck epoch is the first tiny fraction of a second after the Big Bang, and our current physics cannot describe it. Whatever set space-time and the laws of nature in motion lies on the other side of that gap.

This is where science, philosophy and theology meet. In the previous text I imagined a superintelligence, perhaps helped by quantum computers. Suppose one existed. What might it tell us about who, or what, created everything?

First it would have to finish the unfinished work of physics: a "theory of everything" that joins quantum mechanics and general relativity. That is a challenge that has resisted human physicists for about a century, and a superintelligence might solve it, but nothing guarantees it. Only then could it say anything solid about the beginning. Its answer would probably fall into one of three categories.

**Scenario A: the universe could not have been otherwise.** The ASI might show that space, time and the laws of physics needed no creator because they must exist. The universe could have emerged from a fluctuation in the quantum vacuum, from a cycle of universes, or from a higher-dimensional geometry of the kind that string theory or loop quantum gravity describe. In that case the "creator" is not a being but inevitable mathematics.

**Scenario B: we live inside a simulation.** In 2003, philosopher Nick Bostrom, the same thinker behind the superintelligence debate, published an argument that at least one of three things is true: civilizations usually die out before reaching the ability to simulate universes, or advanced civilizations rarely bother, or we are almost certainly living in a simulation. Note that he did not claim we are. A superintelligence, which could simulate whole universes itself, would understand the mechanics of simulation well and might go looking for evidence.

It could look for the equivalent of pixels. If space-time had a smallest unit, a grid at about the Planck scale, that would hint at a resolution limit. In 2012, physicists Silas Beane, Zohreh Davoudi and Martin Savage proposed that if our universe were a simulation running on a grid, the highest-energy cosmic rays might show a directional pattern reflecting its structure. So far, nobody has found such a pattern in the data.

A popular idea says that the universe renders only what we look at, like a video game saving memory, because particles behave differently when measured. That image is fun but shaky. In quantum mechanics, an "observer" does not mean a conscious person watching. It means any physical interaction that records a result. And some people joke about forcing a bug or a crash by concentrating huge amounts of energy in one spot. Nobody knows whether such a thing would exist, or what it would do, so I would not volunteer to run the experiment.

**Scenario C: the answer is out of reach.** If the origin of our universe lies entirely outside it, there may be no trace left inside for any machine to analyze. Even a superintelligence cannot create information out of nothing. It might return something stranger than an answer: a proof that, from within this universe, the origin is computationally unknowable.

## Is the universe fine-tuned?

One more puzzle pushes people toward these questions. Some of the basic numbers of nature, such as the strength of gravity, the mass of the electron and especially the cosmological constant, seem balanced in just the way that allows stars, atoms and life to exist. The cosmological constant is the most extreme case: a simple quantum calculation overshoots the observed value by as much as 120 orders of magnitude, depending on how it is calculated. Not every constant is equally touchy, so claims that "change anything by one part in a trillion and the universe dies" are exaggerated. Still, the balance is striking.

There are two main ways to read it. One is a cosmic lottery: perhaps ours is one bubble in a vast multiverse where constants vary at random, and we find ourselves in a universe that allows observers because we could not exist in any other. This is known as the anthropic principle. The other is that the numbers are not random at all, and that a deeper pattern or design links them. A superintelligence might help decide between the two, or might show that the question cannot be settled from the inside.

## So where does this leave us?

Put the possibilities side by side and each has a name that depends on belief. If the universe is a mathematical necessity, one might say that mathematics itself is the ultimate foundation. If we are in a simulation, one might say the programmer takes the role that religions give to a creator. If the origin is unknowable, one might call that a mystery, or faith. Science can narrow the options but may never close them.

What strikes me most is not any one answer. It is the path itself. A question about the smallest length that can exist leads, step by step, through atoms, space-time, black holes and telescopes, to the oldest question there is. It began with someone asking something they thought was silly.
