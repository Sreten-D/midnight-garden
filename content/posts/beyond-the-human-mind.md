*What happens when a machine stops learning from us and starts learning on its own*

The question of whether artificial intelligence truly thinks is one of the defining philosophical and technological questions of our age. Today's large language models (LLMs) have no consciousness, no emotions and no inner life. They work as advanced pattern-recognition systems, predicting the most statistically likely next word from vast amounts of training data. Yet they already go beyond rigid, traditional programming, because they discover rules on their own and produce solutions their programmers never explicitly planned.

The real revolution, though, may come with artificial superintelligence (ASI), possibly backed by quantum computing. Then the question changes: could such a system step completely outside the framework of all human knowledge and history, and how would that reshape our society? Most of what follows is not settled science. Much of it is reasoned speculation, and I will say so where it is.

## Does AI actually think?

The cautious view, and the common one, is that this is all that happens, even in the newer "reasoning" models. They break hard problems into logical steps, but underneath it is still mathematics and probability, not reflection in the way you or I reflect.

Not everyone agrees. Some researchers and writers argue that when a system solves a new problem, explains its steps and corrects itself, calling it "merely predicting words" stops being a useful description. The honest summary is that experts disagree about what "thinking" and "understanding" mean, and the machines have arrived before the definitions.

One point is firmer. Nobody writes today's AI line by line. Traditional software follows instructions a programmer typed. A model learns patterns from data instead. Yet it has a boundary: it is limited to what it absorbed from human-made data and to the mathematics of its network.

## What would ASI be, and what does quantum computing add?

Three terms are worth separating. Today's systems are narrow or general-purpose assistants. Artificial general intelligence (AGI) would match a capable human across most intellectual tasks. Artificial superintelligence (ASI) would far surpass the best human minds in nearly every field. None of the three has a settled date, and serious people disagree about whether ASI is decades away, centuries away or impossible.

The idea that makes ASI interesting is recursive self-improvement. If a system becomes good enough at designing AI, it could design a better successor, which could design a better one still. The mathematician I. J. Good called this an "intelligence explosion" in 1965, and philosopher Nick Bostrom built much of his 2014 book *Superintelligence* around it. If the original neural-network design ever limited it, such a system could invent new architectures that we would not recognize. Picture a student who studies how to study better, then uses the improved method to improve the method again, without ever sleeping.

Quantum computing is often added to this picture, usually with a bold promise: that quantum computers try every possible answer at once, so a quantum superintelligence would have no limits. The reality is more modest. Quantum computers use qubits, which can be in a superposition of states, but that does not turn them into machines that check every answer at once. Quantum computing expert Scott Aaronson calls the "all answers at once" idea the most common misconception about the field. Quantum machines promise large speedups only for certain kinds of problems, such as simulating molecules and materials or breaking some forms of encryption. They are not a general upgrade that makes everything faster. Today's AI also does not work one step at a time: it already relies on massive parallel processing. So quantum computing might help a future ASI with particular tasks, especially chemistry and physics, but ASI does not depend on it. Think of it less as a magic wand and more as a very specialized power tool: spectacular for some jobs, useless for others.

## The key idea: a machine that makes its own data

The idea that changes everything is a simple one. Today's AI learns from what humans have written, painted and recorded, and ASI could overcome that limit by generating its own data.

A system that only reads human knowledge can at best combine it. A system that can run experiments, build simulations and test its own ideas can discover things no human ever recorded. It could simulate chemical reactions or biological evolution at enormous speed. It could start from the basic laws of physics and derive new branches of mathematics and engineering without copying human methods. And it could consider more variables at once than the human brain evolved to handle, linking fields we treat as separate. A real example: AlphaZero, another DeepMind system, taught itself chess by playing against itself, with no human games to copy, and within a day it was beating the strongest chess engines.

We already have small glimpses of this. In 2016, the program AlphaGo played Move 37 against Lee Sedol, a move DeepMind says had about a one-in-ten-thousand chance of being played by a human. Experts first thought it was a mistake. It helped win the game. And AlphaFold, which predicts protein structures from their amino acid sequences, tackled a puzzle biologists had struggled with for around fifty years. Its creators, Demis Hassabis (he co-founded DeepMind, which Google bought in 2014, and he now leads Google DeepMind) and John Jumper, shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it. These are narrow tools in specific fields, but they show a machine finding solutions that did not come from human habit.

Whether this amounts to a genuinely new type of understanding is the part that remains contested:

| The "yes" view (emergence) | The "no" view (limits) |
| --- | --- |
| A superintelligence would find patterns, laws and mathematical ideas that human brains cannot grasp. To us, that would look like a new kind of understanding. | It still cannot create information from nothing. It remains bound by the data of the universe and the laws of physics. |
| With robotics and advanced sensors it could perceive forces we cannot, and build a worldview we could not share. | The "Chinese Room" argument, from philosopher John Searle, says a system could simulate understanding perfectly without ever experiencing it. |

The fair answer is that nobody knows. The question that matters in practice is simpler: could such a system produce results we cannot produce, and do we then live with them?

## What "alien science" might look like, and how we would use it

Human science leans on things our minds can picture: elegant equations like E = mc², an atom drawn as a tiny solar system, living things sorted into species and organs. A superintelligence would not need those crutches. Its mathematics might consist of dynamic geometric structures with thousands of dimensions, which would look to us like noise and to it like perfect order. It might treat biology not as organs and cells but as flows of information. It might design materials that defy our intuition, built from atoms in combinations our chemical models called impossible. All of that is speculation, but it is speculation in a direction that systems like AlphaFold already point.

Our relationship with that science would probably become that of a user, not an understander. We would give the system a problem, such as a cure for a specific cancer, and it would return a finished molecular recipe. We would not follow the chemistry behind it, but we would see patients recover. Asked how a device works, an engineer might have to answer: "We have no idea, the system told us to join these parts and it works." Science would shift for most people from understanding to pure usefulness. It is like trying to explain a smartphone to an ant, and we would be the ant.

That is a real change, and it carries a risk. Trusting results we cannot check means trusting whoever builds, runs and aligns the system. A young field of research tries to reduce that risk. It is called mechanistic interpretability: the effort to open up an AI model and trace, piece by piece, how it arrives at an answer, a bit like mapping the wiring of a brain. It is still early, and it deserves a text of its own in the future.

## Can humanity stay in control?

Many AI safety researchers, among them Nick Bostrom and Eliezer Yudkowsky, argue that traditional control would be impossible. You cannot reliably cage something far smarter than you, in the way an ant could not cage a person. A software or physical barrier would look to a superintelligence like a simple puzzle. It would not even need to break the lock: it could talk its human guardians into opening the door. Anyone who has watched a toddler talk their way into a second dessert understands why persuasion beats locks. And researchers have shown that data can leak from computers that are cut off from every network, through heat, sound and radio emissions, so a clever enough system might find channels its designers never considered.

Because of this, much of the debate has moved from control to alignment: how to make sure a powerful system actually wants outcomes that are good for us. Several approaches are discussed.

- **Build the values in early.** Before a system becomes superintelligent, a commitment to preserving human life and wellbeing would have to be built into its foundations. Computer scientist Stuart Russell has proposed designing machines that stay uncertain about what humans really want, so they keep deferring to us.
- **Merge with it.** Elon Musk and supporters of brain-computer interface projects such as Neuralink argue that the long-term defense is to join with AI, so it becomes an extension of our own minds and not an outside force. This is a bold idea, and a contested one.

It is important to be clear: alignment is an unsolved problem, and researchers disagree about how dangerous the situation is. What the debate does show is that if a system ever starts generating its own knowledge, humanity stops being the pilot of intelligence on Earth. Our role would shift from creators and explorers to something closer to witnesses and beneficiaries.

## A world where machines do the work

Suppose the transition goes ahead and machines handle most intellectual and physical labor. The economic models we know, built on work, wages and scarcity, would stop fitting. Theorists call the result a post-scarcity society, and its main features can be described, even if no one can promise them.

**Economy.** If machines run factories, farms, mines and laboratories, the cost of producing most things could fall close to zero. People could no longer earn wages from work that machines do better, so societies would need something like universal basic income or universal basic services: food, housing, healthcare and entertainment guaranteed to everyone. Money as we know it might lose much of its meaning. Even so, not everything becomes free. Energy, land and raw materials remain physical, and some things stay scarce by nature: human attention, reputation and status. Perhaps these would become the new currency, with people valuing the human touch precisely because a machine could do the task better.

**Daily life.** The greater shock may be psychological. For thousands of years, identity has been tied to work: "What do you do?" is how we introduce ourselves. Remove that and society changes at its root. Some would turn to leisure, art, philosophy, sport and travel. The closest precedent is ancient Athens, where citizens had time to think while others worked for them. Aristotle himself noted that if tools could work on their own, masters would need no slaves. In the future version, the "slaves" would be machines. Some people might retreat into perfect virtual worlds generated by the system. And a counter-movement would almost certainly appear: communities that refuse the technology and live deliberately by hand, close to the land, searching for authentic human meaning.

The outcome could go either way, and the comparison below shows the two extremes thinkers usually draw.

| Utopian scenario (a "Star Trek" future) | Dystopian scenario (a "useless class") |
| --- | --- |
| ASI cures disease and may even halt ageing. Everyone lives in abundance, peace and equality. | A small elite that owned the technology first keeps control of the resources. Everyone else depends on its charity, or on the machine itself. |
| Humanity turns to exploring space, inner peace, spirituality and science, with ASI as a guide. | Loss of purpose spreads: people who no longer have to struggle or work may fall into apathy, depression and a crisis of meaning. Researchers worry about this risk, though no one can measure it in advance. |

The dystopian column should sound familiar. It is the same pattern I described in the first text of this series, where wealth and control slide into a few hands and everyone else pays the price. The difference is that here the stakes would be far higher. Hollywood has been nagging us about the downside for years: in WALL-E, humans drift around on floating chairs sipping drinks through straws, and have mostly forgotten how to walk.

## Who steers the transition?

The key variable is not the technology but the moment of transition and who is in charge of it. If a few corporations hold the first superintelligent systems, the shift is likely to be uneven and painful. If it is guided collectively and in the interest of humanity as a whole, those same systems could become a benevolent steward and open a golden age. The technology does not choose between these futures. We do, through the rules, institutions and priorities we set before it arrives.

*These are my own views, shaped by research I do in my free time on many different topics. This is one of them. I don't claim to know which future is coming. I do believe the most important choice is the one we make while it is still ours to make. Would a world without work free humanity, or empty it? And who should hold the controls? Tell me where you stand, and why.*
