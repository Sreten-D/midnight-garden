*Or: how the fight against money laundering may have grown into a kind of financial feudalism*

It began with a question I asked almost in passing: what, exactly, is money laundering, and why has an entire apparatus of surveillance been built around it?

Money laundering is the process of running money earned through crime, corruption or tax evasion through legitimate channels until its origin is hard to trace. The Financial Action Task Force sets the international standards, governments write them into law, and banks apply them at the counter: a bank must establish who you are, where your money comes from and where it is going. The justification is serious. Human trafficking, narcotics and the financing of terrorism all depend on turning dirty money into spendable money, and cutting off access to banks makes them harder to run.

I don't dismiss that case. But the more I read, the less the question looked like only a matter of crime. It also asks what gets taken from everyone else along the way, and who gains.

## The money that was never quite mine

Start with a detail I had always taken for granted. Cash in my pocket is mine. Money in a bank account is, legally, a claim against the bank: the bank owes me that sum. The difference is invisible while everything works. It shows when an account is blocked, when a bank flags a customer as high-risk, or when someone asks me to document where my own savings came from.

A bank cannot freeze money on a whim. A freeze needs a legal basis: a court order, an enforcement procedure, sanctions, or a temporary hold on a transaction under suspicion of laundering. The last case is the one that concerns me, because the system can act before any court has heard my side.

There is also the question of who has to explain. In criminal law I am innocent until proven guilty. At the counter, depositing or withdrawing a large sum, I am the one who explains. I wouldn't call that a presumption of guilt, since nobody is convicting me and banks are legally required to check customers. But in practice the burden of explanation sits with me.

Cash is also losing ground. The EU's anti-money-laundering regulation, Regulation (EU) 2024/1624, caps cash payments at €10,000, applicable from 10 July 2027, and requires ID checks from €3,000 in commercial transactions. The cap binds anyone who trades in goods or provides services, whether a company or a sole trader, so a private individual is affected only as a customer. Payments between private individuals are not covered, and the cap does not prevent cash deposits at a bank. Member states may set lower limits. I don't see this as sinister in itself, but the direction is clear: less room to live outside the banking system. Being excluded from that system has real consequences, since without an account you can't receive a salary or pay bills. Critics call account closures de-banking and argue that an account can be shut even when no law was broken.

The strongest fear concerns central bank digital currencies. The worry is programmable money: money with an expiry date, spending restrictions, or automatic deduction of fines. The record is more mixed than the fear. The European Central Bank says the digital euro will never be programmable money, meaning money with built-in rules on where, when and to whom you can pay. It would support only conditional payments that the user sets up, such as an automatic rent transfer. Critics note that "never" is a strong word for software that can be changed, and Singapore and Brazil are exploring programmable money. I treat it as a legitimate concern, not an established fact.

## Tax, and the old question of who feeds whom

A line I have carried for years: the state is the apparatus of the ruling class. It comes from the Marxist tradition, but today it is used most loudly by libertarians and anarcho-capitalists, who go further and call taxation theft.

I don't need to accept the conclusion to see the logic. Tax is the one financial relationship where I can't say no. If I don't buy a product, I simply don't get it. If I don't pay tax, the state can use police, courts and prison. That is why critics describe tax as coercion rather than contract.

A second point puzzles me. The state takes over a service such as education or healthcare, private alternatives become harder to run, and the service then serves as the moral justification for taking my money. I am not claiming this is a plan, only that it forms a loop.

History offers a comparison: what was once forced labor and serfdom is now called a tax obligation. I can't give an exact number of months a year the average person works for the state, because it varies by country and by method. What holds is that income tax, social contributions, VAT and excise duties together take a large share of earnings.

The other side deserves its due. The state provides roads, courts, schools and hospitals. The claim that it "rarely creates value" fits what I see in bureaucratic waste and corruption, but it is too sweeping as a general rule. I treat it as a sharp angle of view, not a conclusion.

## Who pays, and who finds a way around it

The practical question interested me most: does the tax system fall equally on everyone? What I kept finding is that the middle class pays mainly on labor and consumption, while the very wealthy hold their fortunes as capital, which is taxed differently. Four mechanisms stood out.

**Buy, borrow, die.** The wealthy rarely hold wealth as cash; they hold shares in companies they own. Capital gains tax is due only on sale. So instead of selling, they borrow against the shares. A loan is not income and is not taxed, so they can live on borrowing while their assets keep growing. Comparing the loan's interest to the tax is tricky, since interest is a yearly cost and the tax is paid once on the whole gain, but deferral alone is worth a lot.

The biggest effect comes at death. In the United States, heirs receive assets at their market value on the day of death (the stepped-up basis). The debt is not erased, but the tax base is: gains built up over decades are never taxed. An estate tax exists but is rarely paid, because the 2026 exemption is $15 million per person. Some researchers argue this, not the borrowing, is the real loophole. ProPublica's 2021 "Secret IRS Files" series, based on leaked tax records, reported that Jeff Bezos paid no federal income tax in 2007 and 2011 and Elon Musk none in 2018. It also calculated that the 25 richest Americans paid taxes equal to 3.4% of their wealth growth between 2014 and 2018. That "true tax rate" measures taxes against rising wealth rather than reported income. Critics dispute the method, and ProPublica did not allege anything illegal.

**Labor versus capital.** In many countries, notably the United States and parts of Western Europe, labor is taxed more heavily than capital gains. An engineer or doctor whose salary rises moves into higher brackets, while someone living off shares often pays a lower percentage. This is not universal, and not so simple in Serbia. The official figures show why. Salary income is taxed at a flat 10% after a monthly non-taxable amount of RSD 34,221 (2026). Social contributions come on top: 19.9% paid by the employee (14% pension, 5.15% health, 0.75% unemployment) and 15.15% paid by the employer (10% pension, 5.15% health). Capital gains, dividends and most interest are taxed at 15%, and rental income at 20%; capital gains on assets held more than ten years may be exempt for residents. A supplementary annual tax of 10% applies to income above three average annual salaries, with a further 15% above six, and investment income and capital gains are excluded from it. By my own rough calculation, a wage well above the allowance carries about 39% in tax and contributions, measured against the employer's total cost (19.9 + 10 + 15.15, divided by 115.15). Capital income, by comparison, is taxed at 15%, although dividends are paid out of profit the company has already been taxed on. So the pattern holds in Serbia, but it runs through contributions, not through the headline income tax rate.

**Offshore and intellectual property.** Large companies set up a subsidiary in a low-tax jurisdiction and move a brand or patent into it. The parent in a high-tax country then pays that subsidiary large fees for using the brand, reports little profit at home, and the profit accumulates where it is barely taxed. It is legal, but it requires tax advisers a small business can't afford. The best-known version, the "Double Irish", is gone: Ireland closed it to new users in 2015 and ended it in 2020. Profit shifting continues in other forms, and the OECD's global minimum tax is the attempted response.

**VAT and excise duties.** Indirect taxes hit everyone at the till, but not equally. For a family that spends most of its income on food, electricity and fuel, a 20% VAT is a heavy burden; for a multibillionaire it is negligible. VAT is regressive measured against income, less so measured against lifetime spending, and reduced rates on basic foods soften the effect.

## The curious case of the charitable foundation

This was the part that surprised me most, so I checked it most carefully.

I had assumed that when a billionaire "donates 90% of his fortune", the money goes to the hungry and the sick. It isn't that simple. Money placed in a private foundation is not tied to any cause in advance. To keep its tax benefits in the United States, a private foundation must pay out about 5% of its assets a year for charitable purposes, and a shortfall draws a 30% penalty tax. The remainder is invested, and if returns exceed 5% the foundation grows instead of shrinking.

Two things are often misunderstood. Foundations are not tax-exempt: since the 2019 reform they pay a flat 1.39% excise tax on net investment income. And they are not a founder's private wallet. US law bans self-dealing, meaning transactions between a foundation and its insiders, with penalties up to 200% and personal liability for managers who knowingly approve them. Reasonable pay for real work is allowed; private jets and luxury property for personal use are not legitimate expenses. Abuse is possible, but it is not how a foundation normally operates.

So the real story is less dramatic and more serious. The money can't return to the founder's pocket, but it stays under the same family's control, without political accountability. A state would allocate it through a budget and a parliament. A foundation allocates it by its own judgment: to universities, media, institutes. That buys social influence, with a tax break attached. It is not theft. It is private power over money that might otherwise have been taxed and spent publicly.

## Why the system allows it

The simplest explanation I found: capital moves and the middle class doesn't. If a state squeezes a large company or a very rich person too hard, they can relocate headquarters, servers and accounts to a country with a better offer, and states compete for that money by cutting taxes. A salaried worker can't move their home, job and family over a few percentage points. Their income passes through domestic banks, visible and easy to tax. That makes them, ironically, the ideal taxpayer.

I deliberately don't claim that all this was consciously designed. There is evidence of lobbying influence, but intent is something I can't prove. It may be enough to say that laws are written by people with reasons to write them in their own favor, while the rest of us lack the time and money to be in the room.

## What I would change

This section is my opinion, not research. The part of the system I find hardest to defend is the tax on labor, which in Serbia means the 10% salary tax plus about 35% in combined contributions. Taxing the same money when it is earned and again when it is spent looks too harsh to me. I would exempt labor income up to a threshold entirely, so that low and middle earners pay only VAT. Above the threshold, only the income above the line would be taxed, so a raise never costs anyone take-home pay. I think that would be fairer, and it would stop punishing people for working. The obvious objection is funding: taxes and contributions pay for pensions and healthcare, so the lost revenue would have to come from somewhere, and a higher VAT falls hardest on the poor. I don't have the answer yet, but I would rather argue about that than about the current system.

What matters to a worker is take-home pay and what it buys, not the size of the VAT percentage alone. A simple example, with a 30,000 salary and a 20% VAT included in prices (rounded, ignoring the allowance and the employer's share):

|  | 1. Current system | 2. My proposal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salary | 30,000 | 30,000 |
| Tax and contributions on salary | 9,000 | 0 |
| Take-home pay | 21,000 | 30,000 |
| VAT inside what is spent | 3,500 | 5,000 |
| Goods and services actually bought | 17,500 | 25,000 |

The worker pays more VAT in the second case, yet can buy about 40% more. The price is that the state collects far less from this worker, and that gap would have to be filled by higher VAT or by taxing higher earners.

**Progressive or flat?** A side question kept coming up: should someone who earns more pay a higher percentage? In a progressive system, each rate applies only to the income above its threshold, so a raise never leaves you with less take-home pay. Supporters argue that people with higher incomes can bear a larger share more easily, since each extra euro matters less to them than to someone near the poverty line. Opponents answer that it penalizes effort, skill and risk-taking, and prefer one flat rate, where those who earn more already pay more in absolute terms. Serbia sits in between: a flat 10% on salaries, with progressive features only in the allowance and in the surcharge on very high annual incomes. Neither side is wrong on arithmetic. The disagreement is about fairness. My proposal is a third option: no tax up to a limit, and one single rate on everything above it.

An invented example shows how it works. Suppose labor income is untaxed up to 30,000, and everything above that is taxed at a single rate of 20%.

| Annual income | Tax | Average rate | Take-home pay |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 30,000 | 0 | 0% | 30,000 |
| 60,000 | 6,000 (20% of 30,000) | 10% | 54,000 |
| 100,000 | 14,000 (20% of 70,000) | 14% | 86,000 |

Whoever earns more pays more in total, but never loses by earning more. Going from 60,000 to 100,000, the earner keeps 32,000 of the extra 40,000, since take-home pay rises from 54,000 to 86,000. The average rate climbs from 10% to 14%, yet the rate on every extra euro stays at 20%.

## Feudalism in a new suit?

The comparison that kept coming back was feudalism: castles replaced by foundations and offshore structures, serfs replaced by a middle class that pays the bill every year. It is a strong image and also a simplification. Today's system allows change: I can vote, courts and the press operate, and laws can be rewritten. A member of the middle class has far more rights than any serf.

Still, the unevenness remains. The rules that apply to me and the rules that apply to those at the top differ, and that difference is written into law. That is why the fight for financial privacy, through cash or alternative systems, doesn't look to me like support for crime. I'm not naive about it. Cryptocurrencies are not automatically anonymous: many transactions are publicly visible and exchanges require identification. Cash offers one kind of privacy, crypto a different and weaker one. Neither solves the problem alone.

My answer to the question in the title is therefore not a plain yes. It is: partly, but not in the way it first sounded. The best-supported points are unequal access to tax planning and rules like the stepped-up basis. The weakest are the dramatic claims about tax-free foundations and programmable money as an accomplished fact. \
*These are my own views and beliefs, formed by questioning what most of us are taught to take for granted. In my free time I research many different topics, and this is one of them. Free societies are built on open debate, so I put the question on the table: how much of our money and our freedom should we hand over, and to whom? Where do you stand?*
